>A luxury "enthusiast" configuration with two high-end GPUs is not happening on a regular Intel consumer CPU (anything with only 16 lanes). This insufficient PCIe bandwidth is (partly) why the SLI bridge had to be invented. In 2020 PCIe lanes are actually starting to matter for GPUs, so do yourself a favor and stop trying to connect 32 lanes when you only have 16. You need a workstation CPU (and socket) for that (i.e. X-series or Xeon), they are the only ones with more lanes. These should also support ACS directly. Or consider AMD, they have more lanes and PCIe 4.0 (big win once cards support it).

## PCI-e Riser Cable
>If you are fine with a mid-range/budget card for your host (i.e. iGPU is not enough for you or you don't have one or the display ports on your board aren't good enough) and only want to pass down one high-end card (or vice versa, but that would be weird), this is actually doable! Some boards have an x16-sized slot that only offers x4 speed (always, with no influence on other ports). This is how you know the port is attached to the chipset and thus isolated from the other x16 port(s). Four lanes are definitely enough as long as you're not doing heavy gaming. A minor inconvenience is the fact that even low-end cards are always built with a full-size x16 connector. This is mandated by the specification for the sole reason of power management. They wouldn't be allowed to draw enough power on a smaller connector, even if they can't benefit from faster speeds (and many don't even connect all 16 lanes electrically). Enter the world of: Riser cables! PCIe is an astonishingly versatile protocol and will happily run on a smaller number of lanes than originally intended. The cryptocurrency folks are taking advantage of this because their cards don't need to talk much, they have very little bus traffic. So they're actually running double-digit numbers of GPUs on x1 (!) ports through the chipset. The power limit is an issue: Your board may literally catch fire from the out-of-spec power draw if you overdo it. That's why they always use powered risers, typically with a 4-pin Molex connector to supply the required power directly (and safely) from the PSU (although weird constructions like PCIe 6-pin or SATA-power also exist). Note that these are dumb and passive components, they work both with or without the external power connection, but I would only test basic functionality that way. Under peak load you still have a (very small) risk of damaging your motherboard without the power connection. To be fair, the stories of burned boards only happened to people that hooked up 10+ cards or something, so it's very unlikely that you would face any serious issue with just one.
